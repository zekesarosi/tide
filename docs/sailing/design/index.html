Three design directions

We are turning the tide app into a navigation app for sailors and boaters. It keeps everything it already does well: the harmonic tide engine, the storm-surge forecast, the SF Bay live tide map, the Tide Globe and the widgets. Each direction below mocks up the same five screens for the same moment. Halcyon, a 36-footer, is beating out of Port Mira at 9:41 on a Saturday, bound for Sandy Cove. A tug with a tow is closing ahead, and the wind is due to back southwest overnight.

Direction A · dense · instrument-first

Chartplotter Pro

It looks and behaves like a Garmin or B&G plotter: a black frame, six reassignable data tiles, hard soft keys and a true night-red mode.

Strengths

  • Instantly familiar to experienced skippers and racers
  • The densest data: laylines, CPA, a go/no-go matrix and a gate timeline
  • The best night-vision story; night red is a first-class mode

Risks

  • Looks like every plotter, so it hides what makes this app different (the tide intelligence)
  • Intimidating for the casual and new boaters who make up most App Store traffic
  • Far from the existing Slack Water system, so the most new design work

Direction B · glanceable · iOS-native

Calm Modern

Slack Water taken to sea: a quiet map, one sheet with three numbers, Apple-Maps-style guidance, Live Activities and plain-language answers.

Strengths

  • Reuses the app’s tokens, type and components (the tide line, cards, sheets)
  • The fastest answer to “is it OK?”, with the most approachable onboarding
  • Native surfaces (Lock Screen, Watch, StandBy) make the anchor alarm excellent

Risks

  • Can feel thin to skippers who want every number on screen
  • The pale map needs care in full sun and at small scales
  • Tide and current are present but not the hero

Direction C · editorial · tide-forward

Sailor’s Almanac

A cruising guide and an almanac that know where you are: a paper-chart look, a time scrubber on every screen, and passages planned on a time–distance chart.

Strengths

  • Shows off the tide engine: tidal gates, streams and windows become the headline
  • The time–distance plan and the scrubber are genuinely new on mobile
  • A distinctive, premium brand and a natural fit for cruisers

Risks

  • The serif, editorial style asks more effort of the reader underway; there are fewer big numbers
  • The scrubber metaphor has to be learned
  • The heaviest custom rendering (Marey chart, rose, paper chart styling)

Recommendation: build on B, make C’s tide planning the signature, and ship A’s helm mode as an option

Calm Modern should be the foundation. It extends the design system the app already ships (the tokens, SF Rounded readings, the tide line and the sheets), so the sailing app still feels like the same product, and it is the cheapest to build well. On its own, though, it looks like other modern chart apps. What no competitor has is our tide and surge engine, and Direction C is the one that makes that visible.

  1. Shell and everyday screens from B: the quiet map, the three-number sheet, plain-language verdicts, Live Activities and the Lock Screen anchor alarm.
  2. Planning from C: the time scrubber (it extends the existing SF Bay scrubber) and the time–distance tidal-gate chart in passage planning. Pilot-note prose comes from the forecast.
  3. “Helm mode” from A: an opt-in full-screen view with an instrument-tile strip and a true night-red palette. Experienced skippers get density without forcing it on everyone.

Next steps: test the B underway sheet and C’s scrubber on the water in full sun with gloves; validate the night-red palette on an OLED device at minimum brightness; then spec the scrubber as a shared component.

All charts are procedurally generated for a fictional “Kestrel Bay”: the contours, soundings and coastline are illustrative and not for navigation. The tide, current and weather values in the mockups come from one consistent scenario. Fonts are Google Fonts stand-ins: Inter for SF Pro, Nunito for SF Pro Rounded. Each mockup frame is 393×852 (iPhone 16 Pro / 17).
